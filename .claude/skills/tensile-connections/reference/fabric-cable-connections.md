# Fabric-to-cable connections

[V] = confirmed via search excerpt of the cited practice source; [U] = typical practice, verify.

## 1. Catenary edge: cable in a pocket (sleeve)
* The cable slides through a pocket welded along the membrane edge. On larger canopies, **webbing belts parallel to
  the edge take the edge shear** [V, Architen].
* Pocket formed by sealing two halves with a **25–50 mm overlap** at the outer edge [V, Eide terminology].
  Edge cuff cut on a **45° bias** so it can curve without wrinkling [V, Fabric Architecture].
* **The cable must be able to slide.** Asymmetric load and prestressing move the membrane along the cable. A tight
  pocket or high friction puts shear into the edge, which wrinkles it and peels the seams. Control slip by clamping the membrane at the corner
  plate, adding belts parallel to the edge, or adding intermediate edge clamps (PTFE).
* PTFE/glass is abrasion-sensitive. It normally uses clamps and straps; a sleeve only for wide, flat catenaries [V, FA 2014].
  PVC/PES tolerates sliding much better.
* Pocket sizing [U]: inner circumference ≈ π·d + 15–30 mm, i.e. lay-flat width ≈ 1.5–2 d plus the weld. For a 12–20 mm cable
  the finished pocket is about 40–80 mm. It must pass a fitting or have open ends for swaging after pulling.
* Edge geometry: R ≈ c²/(8s); T ≈ n·R; practical sag span/8 … span/15 (≈ 6.7–12.5 %) [V]; designers use 8–12 %.

## 2. External cable with clamp plates (PTFE, large spans)
* The keder-roped membrane edge is held between pairs of short aluminium clamp plates. The plates connect to the cable by bent
  straps, U-bolts or cast cable clamps (Birdair "catenary edge clamping with aluminium straps") [V].
* The flatter the edge curvature, the longer the plates can be; **bolt spacing hardly more than 200 mm** [V, TensiNet].
* Research (ResearchGate 264174342) [V]: large bolt spacing combined with a thin keder lets the keder squeeze into the plate gap and deform unevenly.
  Closer bolts allow thinner plates; welded pockets are less variable than glued ones.

## 3. Webbing (belt) edges
* Belt edge: belts alone form the catenary (small PVC canopies). Belt plus cable: belts take edge shear.
* Belts end at the corner in a D-ring, buckle or **belt tensioner** [V]. TensiNet corner type 5 [V].
* 50 mm polyester webbing is about 27–100 kN MBL [U]. Use a textile factor of about 3–6 and the sewing/weld efficiency.

## 4. Corners where edge cables meet
TensiNet five corner types [V, via BDiR]:
1. Corner plate set apart from the fabric; fabric and cables separately adjustable (PVC, PTFE).
2. Corner plate clamped to the fabric; cables adjustable (PVC, PTFE).
3. Corner plate connected with a keder profile; cables adjustable or fixed (PTFE).
4. Corner plate clamped to the fabric with a continuous edge cable (PVC).
5. Corner plate with belt connections (PVC).

* Acute corners need more rotation resistance and higher forces introduced into the membrane plate during
  prestress; keep enough angle between the edge cables [V].
* **Cut-back (setback)** [U dims]: stop the fabric short of the theoretical node so the terminals, turnbuckles and plate sit
  outside the fabric. Bridge the gap with a membrane plate, strap or adjustable bolts. About 150–300 mm on small canopies, 300–600 mm+
  on large ones, governed by fitting length, adjustment travel and cable angle. Round or truncate the fabric corner.
  Too little cut-back gives radial wrinkles; too much gives a slack corner.
* **Reinforcement** [U]: 1–3 doubler plies welded in, extending about 0.5–1.0 m, warp roughly along the bisector; belts continue into the plate.

## 5. Ridge and valley cables
* Ridge/valley cable over the membrane on a cushion strip, or in a pocket formed at a seam. Check local radius,
  abrasion and clamp slip. Valley cables pull down (hold-down anchors); ridge cables push up (supported on arches or masts).
